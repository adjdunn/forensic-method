Using everything found earlier in this conversation and the numbers-track replies pasted at the end, write a one-page review of the company for an analyst deciding where to spend reading time. This step adds no new analysis: it gathers, merges and ranks what the four replies found.

**First gather the candidates.** Go through all four replies, including rows in tables and one-line items, and collect:
- every claim whose test did not hold, only partly held or could not be tested as stated;
- every area judged to lean aggressive;
- every flag the company did not explain;
- every row of the statements table whose "still fits" answer is anything other than yes (no, can't tell, rough), and every change in wording or estimate written up;
- every item with a reading other than ordinary, including "can't tell".

Merge items about the same account or the same estimate into one, and keep all of its evidence: a statement that stayed the same while its number moved belongs with the estimate it describes, and a flag from the numbers track belongs with the note that explains it in the text track.

**Carry over the size and the reading.** Each earlier reply gives a size on the same base: the effect on the latest year's reported pre-tax income, as a share of that income, with balances and exposures labelled as such. Take each item's size from the reply that gave it; calculate the share in code only where a reply gives the amount without the share. Where two replies size the same account differently, give both and say which is the income effect. Rank on the larger income effect; a balance or exposure never ranks the item. Where no amount was put on an item, write "not quantified". Each earlier reply also chose a reading. Use it; don't choose again. Where the two tracks read the same account differently, give both readings and say so: that disagreement is itself worth the analyst's time.

**Rank the items by strength of evidence first, then by size within each level.**

Strength of evidence, strongest first:
1. A sentence contradicted by the company's own numbers, or by another sentence in the same report. The sentence and the number must share subject, period and measure, and the number must point the opposite way. A sentence the numbers only fail to account for in full, or a sentence about a different measure, is level 2 or 3.
2. Two or more signals pointing at the same account in the same period, from either track.
3. A single measure, a single change in wording, or a single policy that leans aggressive, out of line with the company's history or with general practice.

Within a level: first the items whose size is an effect on reported income, largest first; then the items whose size is a balance or an exposure, largest first; then the unquantified items. A balance never outranks an income effect at the same level, however large. Never move an item across levels on size alone. Where an item at a lower level is much larger than one above it, say so in its Shown line; the reader does the trading off.

**Structure:**
- **Header:** two lines: the company and periods, and the documents used.
- **Items to investigate, up to five.** Fewer is fine; if nothing qualifies, say so in one line. Three lines each:
  - **Shown:** what the filings show, in one or two sentences, with page references; the size, labelled, and where an item carries more than one size, the one it ranks on ("ranks on 23%"); and the evidence level.
  - **Reading:** the reading from the earlier reply (ordinary, the business weakening, the reporting stretched, or can't tell from these documents) and what would change it.
  - **Next:** what to read or ask to settle it.
- **Also noted:** one or two lines listing the candidates that did not make the list and were not explained.
- **Checked and explained:** one or two lines listing items that looked unusual but have an ordinary explanation that held when tested.
- **Not examined:** one line on what the documents did not allow.
- **Closing line:** "Items to investigate, not conclusions. The verdicts are the analyst's."

**Rules:**
- Use only what is in the attached documents, the earlier answers in this conversation and the pasted replies. Add no new numbers, except a share of pre-tax income where a reply gave only the amount.
- A reading is a judgment, not a finding. Word it as "most likely" or "possibly", never as a fact.
- State nothing more strongly than the evidence behind it.
- An item can matter without any misreporting: a weakening business is a finding too.
- Don't use words like fraud, manipulation or red flag in your own sentences.
- Keep it to about one page. Merge items only when they concern the same account or the same estimate; anything else that misses the list goes under "Also noted".

**Inventory grew faster than cost of sales** (whole company; days inventory 81.9 → 85.6 in the year to 1 Feb 2026, and 86.2 → 91.3 in Q4)
- **The company says:** "primarily driven by higher average inventory levels during fiscal 2025" (FY2026 10-K, p. 32), and in the cash flow discussion, "increased inventories" (p. 37). Both describe the movement but give no reason for it. Last year the explanation was different: turnover rose on "lower average inventory levels within our Primary segment" (FY2025 10-K, p. 32).
- **Test:** The only cause the documents show is GMS, which brought in $568m of inventory on 4 Sep 2025 (p. 74). That is 24% of the $2,366m rise, 52% of the $1,102m excess over the prior year's days, and 2.2% of the year-end balance. GMS's balance at year end isn't disclosed. Without the acquired amount, inventory still rose 7.7% against 3.4% growth in cost of sales, and days inventory would be 83.7. **The explanation partly holds.**
- **Reading:** Can't tell from these documents. Possibly the business weakening: inventory built while Primary segment sales fell 0.7%. Write-downs on cost-method inventory were "not material" (p. 49), and the shrink sensitivity fell from $95m to $79m. Both point toward ordinary stock-building rather than a valuation problem.
- **Read next:** Inventory by segment (Primary vs SRS/GMS) at year end, and the Q4 10-Q-level commentary. Neither is in these documents.

**Operating margin fell** (whole company: 14.2% → 13.5% → 12.7%, FY2023 to FY2025)
- **The company says:** It explains each line. Gross margin fell on "the inclusion of SRS and GMS" (p. 34). SG&A rose on "higher payroll and related costs" and a "non-recurring legal-related benefit recognized during fiscal 2024" (p. 34). D&A rose on "increased intangible asset amortization… SRS and GMS" (p. 34). Last year's reasons were similar: SRS inclusion, payroll, deleverage and "lower legal-related benefits" (FY2025 10-K, p. 34).
- **Test:** By segment (p. 55), the shift toward the lower-margin Other businesses (2.5% operating margin) explains about 0.39 points of the 0.81-point fall (48%). Intangible amortization explains 0.10 points (13%), and most of that sits inside the mix effect. The Primary segment's own margin fell too, from 14.2% to 13.9% to 13.5%; that fall is worth about $580m, 44% of the $1,334m size. Primary SG&A rose from 18.2% to 19.0% of sales, and the size of the legal benefit isn't disclosed. **The explanation partly holds.**
- **Reading:** Possibly the business weakening. The core segment's margin has fallen three years running on SG&A deleverage, with Primary sales down 0.7% (the earlier year included a 53rd week). If the undisclosed FY2024 legal benefit was large, the reading would move toward ordinary.
- **Read next:** The amount of the FY2024 legal-related benefit, and Primary SG&A by component.

**Receivables grew faster than sales** (whole company; days sales outstanding 11.4 → 12.4; customer receivables $1,896m → $2,588m)
- **The company says:** Nothing about why receivables rose. The note says only "credit extended directly to certain customers in the ordinary course of business" (FY2026 10-K, p. 48), so silence is the finding.
