Compare the two attached annual reports and find what the company changed in its disclosures, and what it left the same when the numbers moved.

**Part 1: what changed.** Find sentences that were added, reworded or dropped. A dropped word or sentence can matter as much as a new one.
- Ignore changes that only bring dates, numbering or the current year's amounts up to date, or that simply describe a newly acquired business or a new accounting standard.
- Don't ignore a change because the company attributes it to an acquisition. If the wording or the reason given for an estimate changed, it counts.
- A prior-year figure shown differently this year counts as a change.
- Before calling a sentence added or dropped, confirm that both documents contain the section it sits in. One file may be a full report and the other only its key sections. If the section is missing from one of them, say the comparison can't be made; it is not a change.

**Part 2: what stayed the same.** First find every sentence in either report that states a useful life, a holding period or average holding period, a residual or salvage value or a reserve method, and every sentence that gives one of these a direction ("longer", "shorter", "extended", "higher"), wherever it appears: the business description, the risk factors, the management discussion including its expense explanations, the critical estimates and the notes. Search for the words "holding period", "useful life", "average", "residual" and "salvage" and list every hit with its page in each year, so a sentence that was matched but not read is visible. Then, for each statement, calculate in code the number it describes in both years and decide whether it still fits. A stated average that sits inside a disclosed range is tested against the range: an average that reads the same in both years while the top or bottom of its range moved does not fit as stated, unless the report says how the average is computed. A sentence that gives a direction is set beside the depreciation charge and rate for the same years. A statement that stays the same while the numbers around it move is worth seeing. Say so when a test is only rough.

**Part 3: changes the company discloses.** List every change in an accounting estimate or policy that either report discloses, with the effect on income the company states.

**Size** means the effect on the latest year's reported pre-tax income, as a share of that income: the company's stated effect for a change in estimate or policy, or the amount you calculate for a statement that no longer fits. A balance, a commitment or a possible future loss is not an income effect: give it only if it helps, and label it as a balance or an exposure. Where no income effect can be put on a change, write "not quantified".

**Rules:**
- Use only the attached documents. No outside knowledge of this company or of anything that happened later.
- Do every calculation in code. Don't show the code unless asked.
- Quote exactly, with the document and page, for both years. Quote the few words that changed or that carry the point, not the whole sentence.
- Build on your last reply. If a change concerns an item you already wrote up, write up the change in full and add what the comparison shows; don't restate the earlier test.
- Before testing a sentence, say exactly what it claims: about what, over what period, by what measure. Test it only with a number that matches all three. If the nearest number covers something wider (a whole segment for one acquisition, a full year for a part year), say the claim can't be tested as stated.
- Never give an amount only as a share of a small net change. Give it as a share of the total as well.
- A reading is a judgment, not a finding. Word it as "most likely" or "possibly", never as a fact.
- Say what each growth figure covers (the whole company, a segment or a region), and keep to one basis.
- These are things to check, not findings. Don't use words like fraud, manipulation or red flag.
