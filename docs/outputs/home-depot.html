Items to investigate

1. Operating margin and SG&A: Primary SG&A is outgrowing sales, partly explained by an unquantified "non-recurring" legal benefit

  • Shown: Operating margin fell from 13.50% to 12.69%. About 48% of the fall is mix shift toward SRS. Another 43% comes from Primary SG&A, which went from 18.2% to 19.0% of segment sales while segment gross margin rose (p.33–34, 55). Last year the higher fiscal 2024 SG&A was blamed partly on "lower legal-related benefits". This year the fiscal 2024 benefit is "non-recurring", with no amount in either year (p.34 both years). Size: $1,334m, 7.2% of pre-tax income, an income effect; ranks on 7.2%. From numbers-1 flag 2, numbers-2 write-up 2 and text-2 write-up 2.
  • Reading: The tracks differ. Numbers-2: possibly the business weakening, with Primary SG&A outgrowing sales for a second year. Text-2: can't tell from these documents; calling the benefit "non-recurring" could make the cost rise look temporary. If the benefit turned out to be most of the 0.84-point rise, both readings would move to ordinary.
  • Next: Ask for the amount of the fiscal 2024 legal benefit, any similar benefits in fiscal 2023 and 2025, and Primary payroll costs.

2. Acquired intangibles: long lives, and amortization excluded from adjusted results

  • Shown: SRS customer relationships have a 20-year life and GMS's 19, at the top of the "up to approximately 20 years" policy range. Amortization is straight-line even though the valuations assume customer attrition (p.51, 74, 76). The charge does match the stated lives. Adjusted operating income and EPS exclude the $607m of amortization while keeping the related revenue (release p.8). The schedule shows $683m in fiscal 2026 and $6,476m thereafter (p.61). The adjusted EPS gap widened from $0.33 to $0.46.
  • Sizes: income effect, illustrative, of 0.5–1.6% if lives were 15 or 10 years; ranks on 1.6%. Excluded from adjusted income: $607m, 3.3%.
  • Sources: text-1 write-ups 1–2 and profile rows, text-2 statements row and note on adjusted measures, numbers-2 "Adjusted earnings".
  • Reading: For the adjusted measure, most likely an ordinary, fully reconciled choice, slightly stretched because the revenue stays in. For the lives, possibly mildly stretched. Low customer attrition would make 19–20 years ordinary.
  • Next: The attrition and discount rates used for SRS and GMS; the final GMS lives; whether pay targets use adjusted figures.

3. Merchandise inventory: a build the company does not explain, while shrink review moved out of the critical audit matters

  • Shown:
    • Days inventory rose from 81.9 to 85.6, and Q4 from 86.2 to 91.3. Excluding GMS it is still 83.7 against 81.9. The company only restates the movement ("higher average inventory levels", p.32, 37).
    • The store-shrink critical audit matter was dropped (last year p.42); the new matter covers IT evidence for retail-method inventory (p.41–42).
    • The shrink sensitivity fell from $95m to $79m, an implied estimate down about 17%, with the method wording unchanged (p.39 both years).
    • The cost method now reads "moving average cost and first-in, first-out", with no change disclosed (p.38, 49).
  • Sizes: balance of $1,102m in excess inventory (numbers-1 expressed it as 5.9% of pre-tax income); shrink exposure $79m, 0.4%. No income effect. The balance is far larger than item 2's income effect but cannot outrank it.
  • Sources: numbers-1 flag 3, numbers-2 write-up 1, text-2 write-up 1, its shrink table row and its "Other changes" line 1.
  • Reading: The tracks differ. Numbers-2: possibly the business weakening, with stock built ahead of flat demand (comparable transactions −1.0%). Text-2: the change of audit matter is most likely ordinary, and the shrink estimate can't be judged. A deliberate build ahead of tariffs would point to ordinary.
  • Next: Inventory by segment; markdown and shrink reserve movements; how estimated shrink compared with actual counts.

4. Receivables: days sales outstanding rising, an allowance with no figure, and a risk factor now saying Pros "increasingly" use trade credit

  • Shown: Days sales outstanding rose from 7.9 to 11.4 to 12.4; Q4 went from 12.1 to 13.3. Receivables are 3.4% of sales against an industry 2.8%. GMS brought $899m of receivables, which covers the rise. The company explains nothing for fiscal 2025 and calls the allowance only "not material" (p.48). The risk factor changed from "primarily use" to "increasingly use trade credit" (last year p.23, this year p.24).

  • Sources: numbers-1 flag 5, numbers-2 write-up 3, text-1 profile row 2, text-2 "Other changes" line 3.
  • Reading: Numbers-2: most likely ordinary, a mix shift toward distribution businesses that extend credit. Text-1: can't tell, not enough disclosed. Rising days in the Primary business would change the reading.
  • Next: Receivables by segment and an allowance roll-forward with write-offs.

Prompt 2, follow the flags

Inventory: days inventory up from 81.9 to 85.6, with inventory rising 6.7 pp faster than cost of sales (FY Feb-26)

  • The company says: "inventory turnover ratio was 4.4 times… compared to 4.7… primarily driven by higher average inventory levels" (Annual report FY Feb-26, p. 32). That restates the movement; it gives no cause. The cash-flow discussion also only names "increased inventories" (p. 37). Last year the explanation went the other way: turnover rose on "lower average inventory levels within our Primary segment" (Annual report FY Feb-25, p. 32).
  • Test: The note on the GMS acquisition gives $568m of inventory acquired. That is 24% of the $2,366m rise and 2.2% of the $25,817m year-end balance. The cash-flow line, which leaves acquisitions out, shows $1,498m of increase from existing businesses: 63% of the rise, or 6.4% growth on the opening balance. The Primary segment's cost of sales per week rose only 0.4% (52 against 53 weeks). Excluding the GMS inventory, days inventory is still 83.7 against 81.9. The company names no cause, so there is no explanation to test (does not hold). Q4 shows the same pattern: inventory rose 10.1% while cost of sales per day rose 3.9%.
  • Reading: Possibly the business weakening. Inventory built ahead of flat demand, with comparable customer transactions down 1.0% (p. 33). The report also mentions tariff costs and "some price increases" (p. 32). A deliberate pre-tariff build would point to the ordinary reading, but the documents don't say whether there was one.
  • Read next: Inventory by segment and the markdown and shrink reserve movements. Neither is in these documents: the notes only say cost-method adjustments were "not material" (p. 48).

Operating margin: down from 13.50% to 12.69% (FY Feb-26); Q4 down from 11.3% to 10.1%

  • The company says: Gross margin fell on "the inclusion of SRS and GMS" (p. 34). SG&A rose on "higher payroll and related costs… along with the impact of a non-recurring legal-related benefit recognized during fiscal 2024" (p. 34). Depreciation and amortization rose on "intangible asset amortization… from our acquisitions" (p. 34). Last year's report gave the same reasons: "higher payroll costs… and lower legal-related benefits" (Annual report FY Feb-25, p. 34). The Q4 release adds only the 13- against 14-week comparison.
  • Test: The shift in sales mix toward the lower-margin SRS business accounts for 0.39 pp of the 0.81 pp fall (48%). The Primary segment's own margin fell from 13.92% to 13.54%, taking 0.35 pp (43%). The rest of the business took 0.06 pp. Within the Primary segment, gross margin rose from 33.9% to 34.4%, so the segment's fall comes from SG&A. Primary SG&A went from 18.2% to 19.0% of segment sales, growing 3.8% while segment sales fell 0.7%. The company does not quantify payroll or the legal benefit, so that half cannot be tested. The explanation partly holds.
  • Reading: Possibly the business weakening in the Primary segment, with SG&A outgrowing sales for a second year. If the undisclosed legal benefit turned out to be most of the 0.84 pp rise in Primary SG&A, the reading would be ordinary.
  • Read next: The size of the fiscal 2024 legal-related benefit, and Primary payroll costs. Neither is disclosed in these documents.

Receivables: days sales outstanding up from 11.4 to 12.4 (FY Feb-26), after 7.9 to 11.4 the year before

  • The company says: Nothing about the fiscal 2025 increase. The note only defines customer receivables as "credit extended directly to certain customers" (p. 48). Last year's report did explain the rise: "increased… primarily as a result of the SRS acquisition" (Annual report FY Feb-25, p. 48).
  • Test: The company gives no explanation, so I tested the acquisition instead. GMS brought $899m of receivables at 4 September 2025, which is 130% of the $694m rise and 16% of the $5,597m year-end balance. Customer receivables alone rose $692m. Excluding GMS receivables and GMS's $2.0bn of sales, days sales outstanding is 10.5. This mixes an acquisition-date balance with a year-end one, so it is indicative only. Last year's SRS claim also checks out: $1,831m of receivables acquired covers 116% of the $1,575m rise. The acquisition could account for the rise, but the company does not say so.
  • Reading: Most likely ordinary: the mix has shifted toward distribution businesses that extend credit. Rising days in the Primary business would point elsewhere, but receivables are not split by segment.
  • Read next: Receivables by segment, and the allowance roll-forward. The allowance is "not material" (p. 48), and no movements are given.

2. Write-ups

No claim was contradicted, either in the audited statements and notes or in the management discussion. Two areas lean aggressive.

Adjusted measures: a recurring amortization charge is excluded

  • The report says: "excludes the impact of amortization expense from acquired intangible assets … We do not adjust for the revenue" (earnings release, p.8).
  • Test: The reconciliation itself holds. $607m ÷ 995m diluted shares = $0.61, and $0.61 × the 23.9% effective tax rate = $0.15, giving adjusted EPS of $14.69. The charge clearly recurs: the schedule shows $683m for fiscal 2026 and "$6,476" million thereafter (10-K p.61). Size: $607m, 3.3% of pre-tax income ($18,602m).
  • Reading: Most likely an ordinary presentation choice, common among acquirers and fully reconciled. It is slightly stretched, though, because revenue from the acquired customers stays in while their cost comes out. A different reading would follow if the adjusted figure were used to judge whether SRS/GMS earn back what was paid for them. On a GAAP basis, Other earned only $316m on $12.7bn of sales (p.55).
  • Read next: Pay targets tied to adjusted metrics, and SRS/GMS profit before amortization against the $23.1bn paid.

Acquired intangibles: lives at the top of the stated range

  • The report says: straight-line amortization is used "as this approximates the pattern of expected economic benefit" (Note 1, p.51). Yet the customer-relationship valuations rest on "expected customer attrition rates" (p.74, 76).
  • Test: The claim about the benefit pattern can't be tested, because the attrition rates are not disclosed. The charge does match the stated lives. SRS at $346m a year plus about five months of GMS gives roughly $395m, against $398m reported (release p.8). Size, illustrative and not a finding: lives of 15 years would have added about $99m (0.5% of pre-tax income), and 10 years about $300m (1.6%).
  • Reading: Possibly mildly stretched. Under attrition, benefits usually fall over time, so a 20-year straight line most likely pushes expense later. The table labels this choice "leans aggressive", but the effect may be small. If the undisclosed attrition rates are low (sticky trade customers), 19–20 years would be ordinary.
  • Read next: The attrition and discount rates used for SRS and GMS, and how the GMS lives finish once the measurement period closes.

3. Also noted

  • Tie-out gaps: depreciation is $3,514m in the cash flow statement (p.47) but $3,452m in Note 3 (p.58), a $62m gap. Stock compensation is $522m (p.47) against $524m in Note 9 (p.69). The swap liability is $558m in Note 5 (p.64) against $559m in Note 8 (p.69).
  • GMS controls exclusion: the "approximately 1%" of net sales holds ($2.0bn ÷ $164.7bn = 1.2%). The "approximately 3%" of assets can't be tested as stated: the only GMS asset figures are at the acquisition date, and those give 3.8% of year-end assets excluding goodwill and intangibles (p.74, 77).
  • SRS goodwill headroom: Other earns a 2.5% operating margin, and 1.4% pre-tax on the $23.1bn paid for SRS and GMS. It carries $13.8bn of goodwill, tested with the word "exceeded" rather than the "substantially exceeded" used for Primary (p.55, 60).
  • Return on invested capital: invested capital leaves out the $4.5bn of commercial paper outstanding at year-end. Including it gives 24.7% instead of the 25.7% reported (p.35, 61).

  • Before: two critical audit matters. One was the SRS customer-relationship valuation. The other was "Estimation of store shrink", covering shrink "occurring in the period between physical inventory counts and fiscal year-end" and including "a sensitivity analysis over the shrink reserve estimate" (last year's report, p.41–42).
  • After: dropped. The only matter now is "Sufficiency of audit evidence over certain merchandise inventories". It concerns the "highly automated nature of the process" for retail-method inventory, which runs across multiple IT systems (this year's report, p.41–42).
  • Why it matters: the shrink estimate method reads word for word the same in both years: "store-specific basis and is primarily based on recent shrink results" (p.39 both years). The disclosed sensitivity to a 10% rise in the shrink rate fell from "$95 million" (last year, p.39) to "$79 million" (this year, p.39). That implies the estimated shrink between counts fell about 17%, from roughly $950m to $790m (rough). The management discussion credits "lower shrink" for gross margin (p.34). Size: exposure of $79m, 0.4% of pre-tax income ($18,602m); last year $95m, 0.5% of $19,406m. Not an income effect.
  • Reading: Most likely ordinary. Critical audit matters change, and retail-method inventory is still covered under a new angle. The SRS matter fell away because that valuation is finished. A different reading would be possible if the lower shrink estimate came from changing what counts as "recent shrink results" rather than from fewer actual losses. Neither report gives the shrink reserve balance or how far estimates differed from actual counts.

Management discussion, SG&A: the fiscal 2024 legal benefit is now called "non-recurring"

  • Before: fiscal 2024 SG&A rose as a share of sales because of "higher payroll costs, deleverage from a negative comparable sales environment and lower legal-related benefits" (last year's report, p.34).
  • After: fiscal 2025 SG&A rose because of "higher payroll and related costs in fiscal 2025 along with the impact of a non-recurring legal-related benefit recognized during fiscal 2024 within our Primary segment" (this year's report, p.34).
  • Why it matters: Last year's wording implies legal-related benefits in both fiscal 2023 and fiscal 2024. This year the fiscal 2024 benefit is called non-recurring, but neither report gives an amount. It is used to explain an SG&A ratio that rose 0.62 points company-wide (about $1.0bn at fiscal 2025 sales). In the Primary segment the ratio rose 0.84 points, from 18.2% to 19.0% (about $1.3bn). Size: not quantified.
  • Reading: Can't tell. The fiscal 2024 benefit may well have been one-off, and the two sentences don't strictly conflict, since a smaller benefit can still be non-recurring. If legal benefits turn out to arrive most years, calling this one "non-recurring" makes the fiscal 2025 cost increase look more temporary than it is. Pointing the other way would be an amount for the benefit, or a statement that no similar benefits came in fiscal 2023 or fiscal 2025.
